\noindent\textbf{Summary.}
I test whether abnormal English Wikipedia attention to a country predicts the relative weekly
returns of its iShares MSCI ETF, using \NEtfs{} liquid country ETFs over \NWeeks{} weeks
(2015--2026) and a specification frozen before any return was computed. High-attention
countries underperform low-attention ones by \LSLoss\% per year. This spread is not significant
(Newey--West $t=\LSt$): it vanishes once past returns are controlled for, known factors do not
explain it, and \PlaceboPct\% of random placebo signals do as well.
Because attention is short-lived, about \TurnoverPerSide\% of each side is replaced every week
and the strategy loses \NetLoss\% per year after costs. I would not trade it.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Economic thesis}
Do bursts of public attention to a country, measured by the daily views of its English Wikipedia
page, move the price of its equity ETF? I test three
hypotheses, which differ in the sign and the timing of the effect.

\textbf{H0: attention is already in prices.} Page views are public, so professional investors can
watch them as easily as I can; if attention predicted returns, they would already trade on it and
arbitrage the effect away. Abnormal attention then does not predict returns.

\textbf{H1: price pressure.} When a country's page draws unusual attention, we can hypothesise
that inexperienced investors are reading about it to inform themselves before buying its ETF. Their buying pushes the ETF slightly
above fair value, so high-attention countries outperform for a week or two and then give the gain
back \refto{barber2008}{(Barber \& Odean, 2008}; \refto{da2011}{Da, Engelberg \& Gao, 2011)}.

\textbf{H2: risk premium.} Attention spikes often signal a crisis. When a country suddenly looks
riskier, investors sell until the price is low enough that the expected future return pays them for
holding the risk. The country's expected future payoffs are unchanged, but they can now be bought
at a lower price, so the expected return from holding it is higher: high-attention countries earn
\emph{higher} returns afterwards, and the gain persists as long as the risk does.

H0 excludes H1 and H2, but H1 and H2 do not strictly rule each other out: both can be partly true.
The horizon profile of the High-minus-Low spread shows which one dominates: a full reversal points
to H1, no reversal to H2, and a partial reversal to a mix. Existing evidence concerns individual
stocks, using Google searches \refto{da2011}{(Da, Engelberg \& Gao, 2011)} or Wikipedia views of company
pages \refto{moat2013}{(Moat et al., 2013)}; here the question is asked for whole countries, using Wikipedia page
views and tradable ETFs.

\section{Data and signal construction}

\textbf{Data.} Daily English Wikipedia page views come from the Wikimedia REST API
(\texttt{agent=user}, which excludes identified bots), available from 1 July 2015.
ETF prices are daily adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance, so returns include dividends.
The risk-free rate is from the Ken French Data Library; the US dollar is proxied by the UUP ETF.
All data is free and the raw downloads are committed to the repository.

\textbf{Universe.} I start from all \NCandidates{} iShares MSCI single-country ETFs (US
included) and keep those launched before 1 July 2015 whose median daily dollar volume was at
least \$5M over July 2014--June 2015. Measuring liquidity before the sample avoids
selecting ETFs that later became popular. The \$5M threshold is a judgment call: at that
volume a position of a few hundred thousand dollars is below 5\% of one day's trading, and bid--ask
spreads stay within the cost assumption of Section~\ref{sec:perf}; the principle of excluding
illiquid assets follows \refto{novymarx2016}{Novy-Marx \& Velikov (2016)} and
\refto{hou2020}{Hou, Xue \& Zhang (2020)}. \NEtfs{} ETFs pass (\NDeveloped{} developed,
\NEmerging{} emerging markets). The US ETF (EUSA) fails the liquidity rule, and Russia (ERUS)
is missing because its price history was deleted after the 2022 suspension
(Appendix~\ref{app:universe}).

\textbf{Signal.} Weeks run Monday to Sunday (UTC), the time zone in which Wikimedia counts daily
views, so the Sunday cut-off is unambiguous. For country $i$ and week $w$, let $V_{i,w}$
be the sum of the seven daily views of the country's Wikipedia page. Abnormal attention, ASV (``abnormal search volume'', the name
\refto{da2011}{Da, Engelberg \& Gao (2011)} give to the same measure built from Google searches), is
\begin{equation*}
  \mathrm{ASV}_{i,w} \;=\; \ln\left(V_{i,w}\right) \;-\; \ln\left(\operatorname{median}\left(V_{i,w-1},\dots,V_{i,w-8}\right)\right).
\end{equation*}
The log difference makes large and small countries comparable (India's page has six times the
traffic of Chile's), and the median of the previous eight weeks, taken from \refto{da2011}{Da, Engelberg \& Gao
(2011)}, adapts to slow trends such as the secular decline of Wikipedia traffic while ignoring
one-off spikes. Portfolios use only the cross-sectional rank of ASV, so extreme weeks cannot
dominate. Figure~\ref{fig:signal} (appendix) illustrates the construction on an event whose attention
spike is unambiguous, the Brexit referendum: weekly views of the United Kingdom page jump to about
\BrexitRatio{} times their baseline, ASV reaches \BrexitASV{}, and ASV turns negative for a few weeks
afterwards while the eight-week median catches up.

\textbf{Timing and sample.} The signal uses views up to Sunday 24:00 UTC; positions are opened
at the close of the next US trading day and held one week, so there is no look-ahead. The sample
runs from the signal week ending 6 September 2015 (the first with eight weeks of history) to the
week ending 20 September 2026: \NWeeks{} weeks. The full specification, including the robustness
list and the decision rule, was frozen and time-stamped on 3 October 2026 before any return was
computed (Appendix~\ref{app:spec}).

\section{Strategy}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Universe: the \NEtfs{} ETFs of Appendix~\ref{app:universe}.
  \item Every Monday before the US close, download the previous week's (Monday--Sunday UTC) daily
        English Wikipedia views (\texttt{agent=user}) of each country page, plus the eight weeks before it.
  \item For each country compute $V$ = last week's total views, $B$ = median of the eight previous
        weekly totals, and $\mathrm{ASV} = \ln(V) - \ln(B)$.
  \item Rank the $N$ countries by ASV. The $\lfloor N/3\rfloor$ countries with the highest ASV form
        \emph{High} and the $\lfloor N/3\rfloor$ with the lowest form \emph{Low} (8 each with
        25 countries); the remaining countries are not traded.
  \item At the Monday close (Tuesday if Monday is a US holiday), buy every High ETF and short every
        Low ETF, with equal dollar amounts per ETF and equal total dollars on each side.
  \item Hold one week; at the next rebalance close (the close of the first US trading day of the
        following week) repeat steps 2--5 and trade only the differences.
  \item If an ETF is suspended or delisted, remove it from the universe.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Performance evaluation}\label{sec:perf}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the three tercile portfolios and the long--short spread.
The ordering is monotonic but in the \emph{opposite} direction to H1 and H2: High earns
\HighAnn\% per year, Middle \MiddleAnn\% and Low \LowAnn\%. The spread of \LSAnn\% per year is not
significant ($t=\LSt$). The horizon profile (Figure~\ref{fig:main}b) shows
neither the rise-and-reversal predicted by price pressure (H1) nor a persistent positive spread
(H2): no single week after formation is significant and the cumulative spread drifts slowly
downward. Figure~\ref{fig:main}a shows that most of the gross loss occurs in 2022 and 2023 (losses of \LossTwentyTwo\% and
\LossTwentyThree\%), with the trough in \TroughDate.

\textbf{Is attention new information?} A spread can be predictable without attention carrying any
new information. Attention may simply react to past price moves (a crash draws readers), in which
case sorting on attention would only repackage known return patterns such as short-term reversal or
momentum. I check this in two ways (Table~\ref{tab:newinfo} in the appendix). First, each week I
regress next-week returns across countries on the attention rank, alone and then together with past
returns at three horizons, volatility and the baseline attention level, and average the weekly
coefficients (Fama--MacBeth). Alone, the attention coefficient is \FMasv\% per standard deviation
($t=\FMasvt$); with the controls it shrinks to \FMasvc\% ($t=\FMasvct$), so the weak effect picked
up by the sort mostly reflects past price moves. Second, I regress the weekly High-minus-Low return
on four factors built from the same \NEtfs{} ETFs: the market, momentum, short-term reversal and the
US dollar. The intercept (alpha), the part of the spread these factors cannot explain, is
\AlphaAnn\% per year ($t=\Alphat$), and the factors explain only \AlphaRsq\% of its weekly variance
(the regression $R^2$). The spread is therefore neither a disguised factor bet nor new information:
it is mostly noise. The setup can detect a real effect, though: the short-term reversal factor
itself is significant in this universe ($t=\PremREVt$).

\section{Robustness}

A robustness check re-runs the main test after changing one arbitrary choice at a time. The frozen
specification fixed thirteen such variations in advance: the baseline window (R1), the portfolio
construction (R2), the attention page (R3), the sub-period (R4), the trade timing (R6), the
global-fear regime (R8) and the set of countries (R9). A real effect should keep its sign across
them; an effect whose sign changes depends on arbitrary choices rather than on attention.
Figure~\ref{fig:robustness} shows that the sign is negative in \RobNegative{} of \RobTotal{}
variations, but the effect is never reliable. Only the 4-week baseline crosses $|t|=2$
($t=\RoneFourt$), and this is what chance alone produces: when there is no effect, each test still has
a 5\% probability of crossing $|t|=2$, so across thirteen tests about $13\times 5\%\approx 0.65$ such
crossings are expected. The sign flips with \emph{Economy of} pages and in 2020--2021, and the spread
weakens if trades are delayed by one day. If a single country drove the result, dropping it would
push $t$ towards zero or flip its sign; instead, dropping any one country (R5) leaves $t$ between
\LOOtMin{} and \LOOtMax{}, close to the full-sample \LSt. Excluding English-majority countries, whose
pages are read mostly by residents, changes little. Finally, shuffling the attention ranking across
countries within each week 1{,}000 times (R7) produces a spread at least as extreme as the actual one
in \PlaceboPct\% of cases (two-sided $p=\PlaceboP$).

\section{Would I trade it?}
No. Before seeing any result I fixed four conditions that must all hold before trading the signal
with real capital (Table~\ref{tab:decision}). Each answers a question a trader would ask: does the
strategy make money after costs (a), is it stable across periods (b), is it more than a known factor
in disguise (c), and does it survive uncertainty about trading costs (d)? Only (b) passes: the sign is
the same in two of the three sub-periods.

\textbf{Costs make it structurally untradable.} Attention spikes fade within days, so about
\TurnoverPerSide\% of each side is replaced every week, an average holding period of about 1.2 weeks. At 10\,bp per trade in developed and
20\,bp in emerging markets (deliberately conservative, Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}) this costs \CostAnn\% per year: even a correctly signed signal would need
more than \CostAnn\% of gross return per year just to break even, while the whole equal-weighted
universe earned about 7\% per year. Net of costs the strategy loses \NetLoss\% per year
($t=\Nett$, Table~\ref{tab:main}). The verdict does not hinge on the cost figure: at this turnover,
even \BreakEvenBps\,bp per trade would erase the \LSLoss\% gross gap in either direction.

\textbf{Before costs there is nothing to trade either.} The gross spread is insignificant
($t=\LSt$) and indistinguishable from noise: \PlaceboPct\% of placebo signals, which shuffle the attention ranking randomly across countries
each week, do as well. The result is also fragile: the sign flips when attention is measured on
\emph{Economy of} pages or when the sample is restricted to 2020--2021, and the spread weakens when
trades are delayed by one day. Known factors do not explain it either: the four-factor alpha is
\AlphaAnn\% per year and insignificant ($t=\Alphat$), the factors explaining only \AlphaRsq\% of the
spread's variance (the $R^2$ of the factor regression). Finally, no single country drives the result
($t$ between \LOOtMin{} and \LOOtMax{} when any one country is dropped).

\textbf{Comparison with the literature.} \refto{barber2008}{Barber \& Odean (2008)} show that
individual investors are net buyers of attention-grabbing stocks, and
\refto{da2011}{Da, Engelberg \& Gao (2011)} find that a jump in Google searches for a stock predicts
higher prices over the next two weeks followed by a reversal, strongest where retail trading is
high. At the country level I find neither the short-run rise nor the reversal. Three differences
plausibly explain this: country ETFs are large markets dominated by institutions, so retail buying
moves them little; the ETF creation and redemption mechanism ties prices to the underlying stocks,
which absorbs price pressure; and reading a country's Wikipedia page signals general curiosity
(news, travel, sport) far more than intent to buy, unlike searching for a ticker.

\textbf{Next steps.} Two extensions follow from these results: measuring attention on local-language
Wikipedia, which is closer to the domestic investors who trade these markets, and combining attention
with the short-term reversal effect, which is significantly positive in this universe
($t=\PremREVt$).

\section{Deviations, implementation details and limitations}\label{app:deviations}
\textbf{Deviations from the frozen specification.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Decision rule (a) reads ``net-of-cost $|t|>2$''. Read literally, a significantly
        \emph{losing} strategy would pass; I apply the intended meaning, a profitable strategy as
        specified ($t>2$). The verdict is unchanged either way.
  \item Table~\ref{tab:newinfo} adds an ASV-only Fama--MacBeth column as a reference point for the
        specified regression with controls.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Implementation details not fixed by the specification.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item US trading days are days on which at least 90\% of the ETFs have a price.
  \item The Ken French risk-free series ends on 31 August 2026; its last value is carried forward
        for the final four weeks.
  \item Volatility is the standard deviation of daily log returns over the previous 182 calendar
        days, with at least 100 observations.
  \item Transaction costs of 10\,bp (developed country) and 20\,bp (emerging country) per one-way trade are
        assumptions, not measured quotes. They cover commission, half the bid--ask spread and
        slippage, deliberately on the conservative side; short-borrow fees are not modelled and
        would only lower net returns. With a total weekly turnover of \TurnoverTotal{}, the annual
        cost is about \CostMultiplier{} times the one-way cost, so the \LSLoss\% gross gap would be
        erased by a cost of only \BreakEvenBps\,bp per trade, in either direction. The verdict
        therefore does not depend on the exact figure.
  \item The horizon profile reports both event-time returns (week $k$ after formation) and
        overlapping Jegadeesh--Titman portfolios held $h$ weeks, with Newey--West lags $\max(4,h)$.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Survivorship:} Russia, whose ETF was suspended in 2022, is missing for lack of data.
  \item \emph{Bots:} a few spikes have no identifiable news event (e.g.\ Canada, 22 May 2017, 59 times
        the median; Singapore on 24 December of both 2016 and 2017 with near-identical counts) and are
        probably automated traffic misclassified as human. Ranking limits their effect to one week each.
  \item \emph{Declining traffic:} country pages lost a third to a half of their views since 2015
        (search snippets, apps, AI assistants, improved bot filtering). The eight-week baseline and the
        within-week ranking remove common trends, but the signal is noisier in recent years.
  \item \emph{After long events} the eight-week median is itself elevated, so a country is sorted into
        Low for several weeks after a major spike (Figure~\ref{fig:signal}b).
  \item \emph{Language:} English Wikipedia mixes foreign and domestic readers; R9 addresses this.
\end{itemize}
